# Deep Sea Exploration

The deep sea — everything below about 200 meters, where sunlight fades — is the largest and
least explored habitat on Earth. Pressure increases by roughly one atmosphere every ten meters,
so at the bottom of the Mariana Trench, nearly 11,000 meters down, the pressure exceeds a
thousand times that at the surface (https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/facts/exploration.html).
These conditions long made direct observation impossible, and even today more detailed maps
exist of the Moon and Mars than of the ocean floor.

Technology has steadily pushed the frontier. The 1960 descent of the bathyscaphe Trieste first
carried humans to the Challenger Deep, and modern crewed submersibles, remotely operated
vehicles, and autonomous underwater vehicles now survey hydrothermal vents, cold seeps, and
abyssal plains (https://schmidtocean.org/technology/). These expeditions revealed ecosystems
powered not by sunlight but by chemosynthesis, where microbes convert chemicals like hydrogen
sulfide into energy (https://www.whoi.edu/know-your-ocean/ocean-topics/how-the-ocean-works/ocean-zones/).

Deep-sea research carries growing stakes. Vent communities hint at how life might arise on other
worlds, novel deep-sea compounds are being investigated for medicine
(https://www.nature.com/subjects/deep-sea-ecology), and interest in seabed mining for metals
raises urgent conservation questions (https://www.isa.org.jm/deep-seabed-minerals). Scientists
warn that we may disturb these slow-growing, poorly understood ecosystems before we have even
catalogued what lives there.
