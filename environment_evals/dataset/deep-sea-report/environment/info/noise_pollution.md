# Noise Pollution

Noise pollution is unwanted or harmful sound in the environment, typically produced by
road traffic, aircraft, rail, industry, and construction. It is measured in decibels (dB),
a logarithmic scale, and prolonged exposure above roughly 70 dB can begin to damage hearing,
while levels above 120 dB can cause immediate harm. Unlike many pollutants, noise leaves no
residue, but its effects on people and wildlife are cumulative and widespread, especially in
dense urban areas (https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/fact-sheets/item/noise).

The health consequences extend well beyond hearing loss. The World Health Organization links
chronic environmental noise to sleep disturbance, elevated stress hormones, hypertension, and
increased risk of cardiovascular disease (https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/fact-sheets/item/noise).
Children exposed to persistent noise near airports and highways show measurable deficits in
reading comprehension and long-term memory. Because these effects accumulate quietly over years,
noise is often called a "silent" public-health burden that is easy for policymakers to underweight.

Mitigation combines engineering, planning, and regulation. Noise barriers along highways,
quieter road surfaces, low-noise aircraft approach procedures, and building insulation all
reduce exposure at the receiver (https://www.epa.gov/clean-air-act-overview/title-iv-noise-pollution).
Land-use zoning that separates residential areas from industrial and transport corridors prevents
problems before they start, and many cities now publish strategic noise maps to target the worst
hotspots (https://www.eea.europa.eu/publications/environmental-noise-in-europe). Emerging electric
vehicles reduce engine noise, though tire and wind noise still dominate at higher speeds.
